Fairness in Federated Knowledge-Graph Learning

When several organisations train one knowledge-graph model without sharing their data, how the graph is split between them matters a lot. We measured how much, on FB15k-237 with TransE and FedAvg, and found that the wrong split makes the model almost useless.

02The question

Knowledge graphs are often spread across institutions that cannot pool them, for legal or practical reasons. Federated learning lets each client train locally and share only model updates. Most papers treat how the graph gets divided between clients as a preprocessing detail.

We made it the variable. With every hyperparameter frozen, we asked: how does the partitioning strategy change the model's quality, and is the result fair across knowledge domains? A model that works well on film triples but fails on people triples is not something you can deploy.

04Is it fair across domains?

We routed every test triple to one of 10 domains by its Freebase relation namespace and scored each domain separately. Louvain wins 6 of the 10. Pure domain partitioning is close to zero everywhere: every client only sees its own namespace, so it never learns the relations that cross into other domains.

05What overlap does during training

Same 10 domain clients, same 40 FedAvg rounds. The only difference is that in one run each client also receives about 20% extra boundary triples from other domains, with smaller domains getting a bigger share. Without that bridge, validation MRR barely moves off zero.

06How it was built

FB15k-237official splits, inverse-leak-free
PartitionerLouvain · domain · overlap
Local TransE3 epochs per client per round
FedAvg server40 rounds, full participation
Evaluationfiltered MRR, Hits@K, per domain

Fixed setup

  • TransE, 200 dimensions, margin 6, L1 distance
  • Adam, lr 0.005, batch 1024, 50 negatives per positive
  • Centralized: 100 epochs · federated: 40 rounds × 3 local epochs
  • Equal-weight FedAvg, validation-based early stopping, seed 42
  • One script (run_all.sh) rebuilds the whole campaign, about 3 h on CPU

Earlier phases

  • Overlap sweep: Louvain clients with 0–100% edge overlap. With no overlap the federation collapses, and a modest overlap is enough to recover it
  • Fairness audit: per-client and per-domain metrics, plus a q-FedAvg sweep. q-FedAvg did not help, and we documented why
  • Domain-aware overlap: our partition-level fix, which gives smaller domains a bigger share of the imported boundary triples

07Takeaways

  • Split by topology, not by label. Louvain communities with about 20% boundary overlap match centralized training (MRR 0.246 vs 0.240) and avoid the collapse.
  • Semantic silos break federated KG embeddings. Clients that each own one namespace never see the cross-domain relations that people, film and award triples depend on.
  • If the split is forced by policy, add overlap. Domain-aware overlap takes MRR from 0.015 to 0.169, but still sits about 30% below centralized, and that cost should be planned for.
  • Structure explains little on its own. Only node degree correlates significantly with per-domain MRR, and only without overlap (Spearman ρ = −0.73). Whether a domain's relations cross namespace boundaries matters more.

Why this matters for privacy

Federated learning keeps raw triples on each client, but shared embeddings can still leak information. The partition is also where privacy policy gets decided. Knowing what a namespace-bound split costs in accuracy tells you what a privacy-driven design will cost.

Limits & next steps

  • One model (TransE), one dataset, one seed. A RotatE check was designed but not completed
  • Local epochs were fixed at 3 and not swept
  • Next: a differential-privacy extension (DP-FedAvg with clipping and Gaussian noise) to measure the privacy/utility/fairness trade-off
